AI assistants are remarkably capable, yet many people come away underwhelmed. The answer is often generic, too long, or slightly off-target. In most cases the model isn't the problem — the request is. An assistant only knows what you tell it, and a one-line request leaves it guessing about almost everything that matters.

The good news: writing a better prompt is a learnable skill, and it doesn't require special tricks.

## Start with the outcome, not the topic

Compare these two requests:

- "Write about remote work."
- "Write a 150-word internal announcement telling our team that Fridays are now meeting-free, explaining why, and inviting questions."

The first names a topic. The second names an outcome. When you describe what you will *do* with the result, the assistant can make sensible decisions about length, tone, and structure on your behalf.

## A simple four-part structure

A reliable pattern for most everyday prompts has four parts:

1. **Goal** — what you want and what it's for.
2. **Context** — the background the assistant can't know: audience, situation, what you've already tried.
3. **Constraints** — length, tone, things to include or avoid.
4. **Format** — how you want the answer laid out: a table, a bulleted list, a draft email.

<Callout type="tip" title="Example">
"I'm preparing a one-page guide for new volunteers at a community garden (goal). Most are first-time gardeners and many are retirees (context). Keep it under 300 words, friendly, with no jargon (constraints). Use short headings and a checklist at the end (format)."
</Callout>

You won't always need all four, but checking your prompt against them catches most of the gaps.

## Give examples when style matters

If you need a particular voice or structure, show it rather than describe it. Paste a short sample of writing you like and say "match this tone." Examples communicate rhythm, vocabulary, and formality more precisely than adjectives like "professional but warm."

## Ask for questions before answers

For bigger tasks, invite the assistant to ask clarifying questions first:

> "Before you start, ask me up to five questions that would help you do this well."

This turns a one-shot guess into a short conversation, and the questions often reveal assumptions you hadn't considered.

## Iterate instead of starting over

The first answer is a draft. Instead of rewriting your prompt from scratch, give targeted feedback:

- "Shorter — cut it to half the length."
- "The second section is too abstract. Add a concrete example."
- "Keep the structure, but make the tone more direct."

Small, specific edits converge on what you want much faster than repeated fresh attempts.

## Common mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Better approach |
| --- | --- |
| Asking several unrelated things at once | Split them into separate requests |
| Leaving out the audience | Say who will read or use the result |
| Accepting facts without checking | Verify names, numbers, dates, and quotes |
| Pasting sensitive data | Remove personal or confidential details first |

## Know where judgment still matters

Assistants can draft, summarize, brainstorm, and restructure quickly, but they can also produce confident-sounding errors. Treat the output like work from a capable new colleague: useful, often excellent, and always worth reviewing before it goes out under your name. For anything factual, see our guide on [fact-checking AI-generated answers](/blog/fact-check-ai-answers).

## A quick checklist

Before you send a prompt, ask yourself:

- Did I say what the result is *for*?
- Did I include context the assistant can't guess?
- Did I specify length, tone, and format?
- Have I removed anything private?

Four quick checks, and the answers you get will be noticeably more useful.
