Time blocking is a simple idea: instead of working from an endless to-do list, you assign tasks to specific blocks of time on your calendar. A to-do list tells you *what* to do. A time-blocked calendar also tells you *when* — and makes it obvious when you've planned more than fits.

The catch is that strict time blocking can collapse the first time a meeting runs over. This guide focuses on a forgiving version built for real schedules.

## Why time blocking works

- **It makes trade-offs visible.** When every task needs a slot, you see quickly that you can't do everything today.
- **It protects focus.** A block labeled "Draft report" is harder to fill with casual meetings than empty space.
- **It reduces decision fatigue.** You decide what to work on once, during planning, instead of all day long.

## Step 1: Start with fixed commitments

Open your calendar for tomorrow and note everything that's already fixed: meetings, appointments, school runs, commute time. What remains is the time you actually have to work with — often less than you'd expect.

## Step 2: Block your most important work first

Choose one or two important tasks and place them in your best hours. For many people that's the morning, but use whatever time you tend to have the most energy.

<Callout type="tip" title="Start small">
In your first week, block just one focus session per day. Success with one block builds the habit faster than a perfectly planned day that falls apart.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Batch the small stuff

Email, messages, quick admin, and errands fit well into one or two "shallow work" blocks. Batching keeps them from fragmenting your focus blocks.

## Step 4: Add buffers

This is the step most people skip — and the reason most time-blocking attempts fail. Leave unscheduled buffer time between blocks and at least one larger buffer in the afternoon. When something overruns, the buffer absorbs it instead of the whole day shifting.

## Step 5: Reschedule, don't abandon

When the plan breaks (it will), take two minutes to move the remaining blocks. A rescheduled plan is still a plan. The point isn't to follow the calendar perfectly; it's to make intentional decisions when things change.

## An example day

| Time | Block |
| --- | --- |
| 9:00–10:30 | Focus: project proposal |
| 10:30–11:00 | Buffer |
| 11:00–12:00 | Meetings |
| 12:00–13:00 | Lunch |
| 13:00–13:45 | Shallow work: email and messages |
| 13:45–15:15 | Focus: data review |
| 15:15–16:00 | Buffer / overflow |
| 16:00–16:30 | Shallow work and plan tomorrow |

## Review at the end of the week

Spend a few minutes each Friday looking back. Which blocks happened? Which got pushed? Did you consistently underestimate certain tasks? These observations make next week's plan more realistic. Our guide to [the weekly review](/blog/weekly-review-habit) shows a simple way to do this.

## Common pitfalls

- **Overfilling the day.** If every minute is blocked, any surprise causes collapse.
- **Blocks that are too vague.** "Work on project" is easy to drift through; "Write section two of proposal" is not.
- **Never protecting blocks.** Treat important focus blocks like meetings with yourself — decline conflicts when you can.

Time blocking isn't about rigid control. It's about giving your priorities a real place in the day, with enough flexibility to survive the day as it actually happens.
