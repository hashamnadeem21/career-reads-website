We make countless decisions every day, most of them small and automatic. Every so often a decision deserves more care: a job offer, a big purchase, a move, or simply what to drop from an overloaded week. Simple frameworks don't make decisions for you, but they help you think more clearly and avoid common traps.

## First, decide how much the decision deserves

Not every decision needs a framework. A useful question is whether a choice is easy to reverse.

- **Reversible decisions** — trying a new tool, a restaurant, a weekly routine — can be made quickly. If it doesn't work, change course.
- **Hard-to-reverse decisions** — signing a long contract, relocating, making a large purchase — deserve more time and information.

Spending an hour agonizing over a reversible choice is a cost in itself.

## Weighted pros and cons

A plain pros-and-cons list treats every item as equal, which rarely reflects reality. Try weighting them:

1. List the factors that matter (salary, commute, growth, team, flexibility).
2. Give each factor a weight from 1 to 5 based on how much it matters to you.
3. Score each option on each factor from 1 to 5.
4. Multiply and add up the scores.

| Factor | Weight | Option A | Option B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Commute | 4 | 2 (8) | 5 (20) |
| Growth | 5 | 5 (25) | 3 (15) |
| Flexibility | 3 | 3 (9) | 4 (12) |
| **Total** | | **42** | **47** |

The total isn't a verdict. Its main value is that it makes your priorities explicit — and if the "winning" option feels wrong, that's a signal a factor is missing.

## The pre-mortem

Before committing, imagine it's a year from now and the decision has turned out badly. Ask: *what went wrong?* Write down the most likely reasons. This flips your perspective and often surfaces risks you'd overlooked. You can then decide whether to mitigate them or choose differently.

<Callout type="tip" title="Use it with others">
Pre-mortems work well in teams. Asking everyone to imagine failure makes it easier to raise concerns without seeming negative.
</Callout>

## The 10/10/10 rule

Popularized by writer Suzy Welch, this simple question helps balance short- and long-term feelings: *How will I feel about this decision in 10 minutes, 10 months, and 10 years?* It's especially useful when short-term discomfort is pulling you away from something you value.

## Set a decision deadline

Open-ended decisions drain energy. Give yourself a deadline: "I'll decide by Friday at 5 p.m." Gather information until then, then choose based on what you know. Waiting for perfect information usually means waiting forever.

## Reduce small decisions

Decision-making takes effort. Routines reduce the number of small choices you make — a standard breakfast, a weekly meal plan, a [weekly review](/blog/weekly-review-habit) — freeing energy for decisions that matter.

## A quick decision checklist

- Is this reversible? If so, decide quickly.
- What really matters here? Weight it.
- What could go wrong? Run a pre-mortem.
- How will I feel later? Try 10/10/10.
- When will I decide? Set a deadline.

Better decisions rarely come from a single moment of brilliance. They come from a calmer, more structured way of thinking — and that's something anyone can practice.
