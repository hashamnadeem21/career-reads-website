Slow Wi-Fi is one of the most common household tech frustrations, and it's often fixable without buying anything. The key is to troubleshoot in order, so you find the real cause instead of guessing.

## Step 1: Find out where the slowdown is

First, separate a Wi-Fi problem from an internet problem.

1. Run a speed test on a device connected by cable to the router, if you can.
2. Run the same test over Wi-Fi standing next to the router.
3. Run it again in the room where things feel slow.

If the wired test is slow too, the issue is likely your internet connection or plan — contact your provider. If it's fast near the router but slow elsewhere, the problem is Wi-Fi coverage, and the rest of this guide applies.

## Step 2: Restart and update

It's a cliché because it works. Restart the router and modem. Then sign in to the router's settings or app and check for a firmware update. Updates often fix stability bugs and security issues.

## Step 3: Move the router

Router placement has a large effect on coverage. Wireless signals weaken as they pass through walls, floors, and large objects.

- Place the router centrally and in the open, not in a cupboard or behind a TV.
- Raise it off the floor — a shelf works well.
- Keep it away from microwaves, cordless phone bases, and thick masonry where possible.

<Callout type="tip" title="Quick test">
Try a temporary location for a day before drilling holes or running cables. Repeat your speed test in the same rooms to compare.
</Callout>

## Step 4: Use the right band

Most modern routers broadcast on multiple frequency bands:

| Band | Strength | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.4 GHz | Travels farther and through walls better | Slower and more crowded |
| 5 GHz | Faster | Shorter range |
| 6 GHz (on newer equipment) | Fast and less crowded | Shortest range; needs compatible devices |

If a device is close to the router, a higher band usually gives better speed. Distant devices may do better on 2.4 GHz.

## Step 5: Reduce congestion

In apartment buildings, many networks compete for the same channels. Most routers can choose channels automatically, but if yours is set manually, switching to automatic — or trying a less-crowded channel — can help.

Also check what's using your bandwidth. Large cloud backups, game downloads, and multiple video streams at once can make everything else feel slow.

## Step 6: Consider extending coverage

If one router simply can't reach every room, consider:

- **A mesh system**, which uses several units that work together as one network. Good for larger homes or awkward layouts.
- **A wired access point**, if you can run an Ethernet cable to the far side of the home. Often the most reliable option.
- **Powerline adapters**, which use your electrical wiring — results vary with your home's wiring.

## Step 7: Know when to upgrade

If your router is many years old, it may not support newer Wi-Fi standards your devices can use. Upgrade after you've ruled out placement and congestion, not before — a new router in a bad spot will still struggle.

## Summary

Test to find where the problem is, restart and update, improve placement, choose the right band, reduce congestion, and only then spend money. Most homes see a noticeable improvement before step six.
