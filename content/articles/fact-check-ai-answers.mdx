AI assistants are fluent by design. They produce well-structured, confident prose whether the underlying claim is solid or invented. That fluency is useful — and it is also exactly why a quick verification habit matters.

You don't need to fact-check every sentence. You need a fast way to spot the claims that carry risk and confirm them before you rely on them.

## Why confident answers can still be wrong

Language models generate text by predicting likely sequences of words. They are not looking facts up in a database unless a tool connects them to one. As a result, they can:

- blend details from similar but different sources,
- produce plausible-looking citations that don't exist,
- give figures that are outdated or simply wrong,
- attribute quotes to the wrong person.

None of this means the tools are useless. It means the output is a starting point for checking, not the end of it.

## Step 1: Highlight the load-bearing claims

Read the answer once and mark every statement that would matter if it were wrong. These usually fall into a few groups:

- **Numbers** — prices, statistics, dates, measurements.
- **Names** — people, organizations, products, laws.
- **Quotes** — anything presented as someone's exact words.
- **Causal claims** — "X causes Y," "this reduces risk by…"
- **Instructions** — especially legal, medical, financial, or safety steps.

General explanations ("a router directs traffic between networks") need less scrutiny than specific claims ("this model supports a particular standard").

## Step 2: Find a primary source

For each load-bearing claim, look for the original source rather than another article repeating it:

- For statistics, find the organization that collected the data.
- For product features, check the manufacturer's official documentation.
- For laws and regulations, use the government's own publication.
- For research findings, read at least the abstract of the actual study.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check citations, too">
If an assistant gives you a link or reference, open it. Confirm that it exists and that it actually says what the answer claims it says.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Check numbers in context

A number can be accurate and still misleading. Ask:

- What year does this figure come from?
- Is it a total, an average, or a median?
- Who measured it, and how?
- Does the source define terms the same way the answer does?

## Step 4: Verify quotes word for word

Search for a distinctive phrase from the quote in quotation marks. If you can't find it in a reliable original source, don't present it as a quote. Paraphrase the idea and credit the source you *can* confirm.

## Step 5: Ask the assistant to show its uncertainty

Follow-up prompts can surface weak spots:

> "Which parts of your answer are you least certain about?"
>
> "List the specific claims in your answer that I should verify independently."

The response isn't proof of accuracy, but it is a useful map of where to look first.

## When to stop and ask an expert

Some decisions deserve a qualified human: medical symptoms, legal disputes, tax filings, investments, and safety-critical work. Use AI to prepare better questions for that conversation, not to replace it.

## A five-minute routine

1. Mark the load-bearing claims.
2. Find a primary source for each.
3. Check dates and definitions on any numbers.
4. Confirm quotes word for word.
5. Note anything you couldn't verify — and leave it out or flag it.
